DNA methylation: an SN2 reaction that controls your genes

Background for the simulation

1 · DNA in one paragraph

Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) is a long polymer. Its backbone is made of sugar and phosphate groups, and each sugar carries a nitrogen-containing ring called a base. There are four bases: adenine (A), guanine (G), cytosine (C) and thymine (T). Two strands wind into a double helix, held together by hydrogen bonds between base pairs: A pairs with T, and G pairs with C. The order of the bases spells out the cell's genes.

The enzyme surface and the DNA next to it come from the X-ray crystal structure of DNMT1 caught in the act of methylating DNA (Protein Data Bank entry 4DA4, ref. 2); the DNA is extended beyond the crystal with the same helix geometry. DNA atoms are drawn as spheres, with each base tinted by its color in the key. The crystal is of mouse DNMT1, whose residue numbers are 3 higher than the human numbers used in the labels (e.g., mouse Cys1229 = human Cys1226).

2 · Methylation is an SN2 reaction

Cells can attach a methyl group (CH₃) to carbon 5 of cytosine, making 5-methylcytosine (5mC). The methyl group comes from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), which has a CH₃ bonded to a positively charged sulfur. SAM is essentially nature's version of methyl iodide (CH₃I):

  • Substrate: the methyl carbon, which is unhindered and so ideal for SN2.
  • Leaving group: a neutral sulfide, S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH). It is a good leaving group because the sulfur gets rid of its positive charge when it leaves.
  • Nucleophile: C5 of cytosine. This is the hard part, because C5 sits in an electron-poor aromatic ring and is a very weak nucleophile on its own.

DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) is the enzyme (a protein catalyst) that performs this reaction. To make C5 a better nucleophile, it first bonds a sulfur atom from its own amino acid side chain (cysteine 1226, or Cys1226) to the neighboring carbon, C6. That pushes electron density onto C5. C5 then attacks the CH₃ of SAM by backside attack: the C–S bond breaks as the new C–C bond forms, and the methyl's three H's flip like an umbrella (inversion). Finally, a base in the active site removes the extra H from C5, the enzyme lets go, and the aromatic ring is restored. The REACTION MECHANISM tab shows each step with curved arrows.

3 · Epigenetics: switching genes off

A methyl group doesn't change the base sequence; 5mC still pairs with G. But methylation acts like a chemical "off switch." It happens mainly at CpG sites, where a C is followed by a G on the same strand. When many CpG sites near the start of a gene are methylated, the cell can no longer read (express) that gene. Information stored on top of the DNA sequence like this is referred to as epigenetic ("above the genes"). This is how a skin cell and a nerve cell can share identical DNA yet use different genes.

When a cell copies its DNA before dividing, the new strand starts out unmethylated. DNMT1 travels along behind the copying machinery and methylates the new strand to match the old one, so both daughter cells inherit the same pattern of switched-off genes.

4 · When methylation goes wrong: cancer

Cancer cells often have scrambled methylation patterns:

  • Too much methylation in the wrong places. Tumor-suppressor genes are genes that normally put the brakes on cell division or repair damaged DNA. When the CpG sites at the start of these genes become heavily methylated, the genes are switched off. Without their brakes, cells can divide uncontrollably. Examples include DNA-repair genes such as MLH1 in some colon cancers and BRCA1 in some breast cancers.
  • Too little methylation elsewhere. Losing methylation across large regions of the genome makes DNA less stable and can switch on genes that should stay silent.

Unlike a mutation, methylation can be reversed. That makes DNMT1 a drug target, and the module at the end of the simulation shows how the cancer drug azacitidine traps DNMT1 to undo this silencing.

Azacitidine: an irreversible inhibitor of DNMT1

Understanding the mechanism of action of an anticancer drug

DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) keeps the cell's methylation pattern intact. Each time a cell copies its DNA, DNMT1 adds CH₃ groups to the new strand so it matches the old one. Cells use this methylation to control which genes are expressed: when the CpG sites (a C followed by a G) near the start of a gene are methylated, that gene is switched off.

In many cancers this system goes wrong. The CpG sites at the start of tumor-suppressor genes, which normally slow cell division or repair damaged DNA, become overmethylated, switching off the cell's brakes. DNMT1 then faithfully copies this abnormal pattern into every daughter cell. Blocking DNMT1 can allow the silenced genes to switch back on.

Azacitidine (5-azacytidine) is the nucleoside cytidine with a nitrogen in place of carbon at position 5 of the ring. Cells phosphorylate it; most ends up in RNA, but roughly 10–20% is converted to the deoxy form and built into DNA in place of cytosine. Its close relative decitabine (5-aza-2′-deoxycytidine) goes straight into DNA.

When DNMT1 meets a 5-azacytosine at a hemimethylated CpG, it runs its normal chemistry: Cys1226 bonds to C6 and the methyl group goes to N5. But the step that releases the enzyme removes the H from position 5, and N5 has no H. The Cys–C6 bond cannot break, so DNMT1 stays covalently trapped on the DNA. This is irreversible, mechanism-based ("suicide") inhibition.

Consequences. Trapped DNMT1 is tagged and destroyed by the proteasome (a large protein complex that acts as the cell's "shredder," breaking down proteins marked for disposal). As cells keep dividing without enough DNMT1, new strands stay unmethylated (passive demethylation), and silenced genes can switch back on. At high doses the bulky protein–DNA crosslinks also damage DNA and kill cells.

Where it comes from. Azacitidine was first synthesized in 1964 by Pískala and Šorm in Czechoslovakia, and was soon also found as a natural antibiotic made by the soil bacterium Streptoverticillium ladakanus. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved it (Vidaza) in 2004 as the first drug for myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS), a group of bone-marrow cancers in which blood-forming stem cells develop abnormally and fail to make enough healthy blood cells. MDS can progress to acute myeloid leukemia (AML), which azacitidine is also used to treat, including as an oral tablet (Onureg, 2020).
